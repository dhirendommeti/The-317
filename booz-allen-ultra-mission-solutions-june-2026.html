Booz Allen's $720 Million Shortcut

Booz Allen Hamilton agreed to buy Ultra I&C Mission Solutions from Cobham Ultra Group. The deal is less about capability and more about time — and what it reveals about where defense tech M&A is headed.

By Dhiren Dommeti · June 23, 2026 · 6 min read · Defense · M&A

Building an encryption division takes years. Acquiring one takes a wire transfer.

Booz Allen Hamilton announced Monday it would buy Ultra I&C Mission Solutions from Cobham Ultra Group for $720 million — picking up a defense technology business that already has engineers with clearances, contracts with the Army, Air Force, and Navy, and products deployed in the field. The deal is expected to close by September.

What Booz Allen actually bought is time.

The company's backlog hit $38 billion last quarter. A 1.1x book-to-bill ratio. Demand is not the constraint. The constraint is having the right products sitting under the right contract vehicles fast enough to capture it. Ultra Mission Solutions — which makes mission software, ruggedized edge processors, and encryption management tools for battlefield communications — fits that gap directly.

The Advent side of this

The seller is doing something worth watching separately.

Advent International spent roughly $8 billion assembling a defense electronics platform — buying Cobham in 2020 for £4 billion, then Ultra Electronics two years later for £2.5 billion. It was a big bet on Western rearmament. The bet paid. Now Advent is cutting the platform into pieces and selling each one to the strategic buyer best positioned to scale it.

March: Ultra Cyber goes to Airbus Defence and Space. June: Ultra Mission Solutions goes to Booz Allen.

"Ultra Mission Solutions has established itself as a trusted partner to the U.S. military and its allies with a portfolio of capabilities designed for the next generation of national security missions."
— Mike Marshall, Managing Director, Advent International

That's not a fire sale. That's a playbook. Find the right strategic home for each asset, collect a premium from buyers who can plug the product into existing contract vehicles, and exit cleanly. Marshall called Booz Allen "the right home to scale that vision further." That's exit language, but it's accurate exit language.

The part that doesn't get written about

Booz Allen is a 34,000-person firm. Ultra Mission Solutions has 220 employees, 135 of them specialized engineers who built focused products inside a focused company.

Large defense primes buy small technical shops all the time. Sometimes the engineers stay, the products get integrated, and the acquisition looks smart in five years. Sometimes the culture dissolves, the engineers leave, and the buyer ends up with contracts but no one who understands the technology well enough to win the follow-on work.

Booz Allen paid a price that assumes the former. The next few years will say which one it gets.

Why this deal is bigger than $720 million

The defense tech M&A market has been moving in one direction for three years. Strategics are winning. PE is selling.

The reason is structural. A financial sponsor can own an encryption company and run it well. But it can't plug that company's products into a $38 billion contract backlog on day one. Booz Allen can. That's not a competitive advantage — it's a different category of buyer entirely. And sellers, and their bankers, have figured that out.

What you're seeing in the Cobham Ultra carve-outs isn't Advent struggling to find exits. It's Advent running a disciplined process where each business unit goes to whoever can generate the most value from it — and collecting accordingly. That's how the best PE firms operate at the end of a hold period. The $720 million Booz Allen is paying for 220 people and three product lines reflects that leverage.

What to Watch · Key Takeaways

  • The close is targeted for September 30, 2026 — pending Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust clearance. No major obstacles are expected given the complementary nature of the two businesses.
  • Booz Allen was awarded a Space Force contract under the Golden Dome program in May. Ultra Mission Solutions' edge compute and encryption stack slots directly into that kind of program.
  • Advent still holds other Ultra Electronics assets. The pace of remaining exits will signal how much dry powder the firm is returning to LPs — and whether a broader realization event is coming.
  • The integration question is real. Ultra Mission Solutions operates across five facilities with a tight engineering headcount. Retention packages and operational autonomy will determine whether the acquisition holds its value.
  • For defense tech M&A broadly, this deal reinforces a pattern: strategics with existing DoD contract vehicles are outcompeting PE for technical assets. Expect more carve-outs like this in 2026 and into 2027.

There will be more deals like this. The DoD's push toward AI-enabled battlefield systems, resilient communications, and hardened edge infrastructure is not slowing down. The companies that move first lock in the contract vehicles. The ones that wait buy at a higher price, or don't get to buy at all.

Booz Allen moved first. Whether $720 million was the right price for that position is a question the September close will start to answer — and the integration will finish.
