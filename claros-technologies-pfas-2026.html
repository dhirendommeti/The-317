Claros' $55 Million Bet on Permanent Treatment

The Minnesota-based startup raised a $55 million Series B round to scale PFAS destruction technologies, highlighting increasing investor interest in one of environmental remediation's growing markets

By Ashrith Desu · 2026 · 4 min read · Venture · Environmental

Claros Technologies announced a $55 million Series B financing, with the goal of accelerating its commercialization of PFAS destruction technology (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances), expand operations, and increase overall manufacturing capacity.

The round was led by Treehouse, with Daikin America, Veralto, Bush Foundation, Nord Asset Management, and others participating. The biggest rationale for the financing seems to be the bet on an increasingly expensive regulatory problem.

Based in Minneapolis, Claros developes technologies th destroy PFAS, a class of synthetic compounds known as "forever chemicals", known to persist in the environment for decades. Their current platform combines:

Opposed to other remediation solutions that can capture PFAS for disposal later, Claros focuses on permanently destroying those compounds. The company claims that their platform can eliminate long, short, and ultra-short-chain compounds while detecting and measuring contamination through lab services.

Why This Deal is Significant

PFAS contamination has become one of the fastest-growing environmental challenges. As regulatory scrutiny increases, organizations are shifting their focus to permanently treating these issues, rather than simple detection, which is what Claros is pursuing.

Serving industries where PFAS remain critical to manufacturing, Claros allows customers to continue using these chemicals while addressing disposal and remediation challenges.

Additionally, the funding is not the only backbone of the deal. It's their customer base.

Claros isn't building a climate-tech company for consumers. Its customers range from manufacturers, to remediation contractors, utilities, and agencies, and all these customers are facing this regulatory pressure to address PFAS contamination. That demand is so resilient to economic cycles and driven more by environmental compliance, industry sentiment, and public policy.

As governments tighten PFAS regulations, spending on permanent treatment is likely to become increasingly non-discretionary.
